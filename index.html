Why Is My Mint Ice Cream Less Green?

America’s Food Is Changing Color

By Cynthia2 min read

SAN FRANCISCOIn 2018, Chronicle staff building a papier-mâché It’s-It matched paint chips to its mint. This spring that green disappeared, and the filling inside the wrapper is now a milky jade.

The company’s president told the Chronicle the peppermint is untouched, and that his technicians, checking “which one would give color without an aftertaste,” swapped FD&C Blue No. 1 for spirulina, an algae pigment.

Six dyes ran the grocery aisle

Each jar is one synthetic dye. The pipes lead to foods FDA lets it color.

Spread

FDA lets Blue No. 1 color candy, drinks, cereal, ice cream and frosting. Each of the six dyes came in one fixed shade, so a cereal plant in Minnesota and an ice cream plant near San Francisco could pour from the same jar and get the same color every time.

2026

This year the machine cracked. All six dyes are still legal nationwide, yet companies are dropping them anyway, pushed by federal health officials and by a California law that keeps them out of school meals from the end of 2027.

Split

Below the crack each dye breaks into a handful of plant recipes, every food lands a little off from where it used to sit, and the candy shells that leaned on the two blues have nothing to catch yet.

The thin outline around each food is its old color.

Note: food colors are drawn, not measured. Sources: FDA color additive rules; San Francisco Chronicle, 2026; Mars, 2026.

One frosted cookie, food makers told NPR, needed thirty times more natural color just to look as bright.

How do you make mint green?

Ice cream starts out white, and nothing in peppermint is green, so mint gets its color the way a kid mixes paint: a blue laid over a yellow-orange until the overlap turns green.

Overlap

For decades the blue was Blue No. 1 and the yellow-orange was annatto, ground from the seeds of the achiote tree and the same color that turns cheddar orange. Where the two circles cross is the bright mint Bay Area freezers knew.

Swap

Swap in spirulina, a blue pressed out of algae, and the overlap washes pale. It’s-It hasn’t named the rest of its new blend, so the annatto circle gets a question mark. Tap the sandwich and take a bite, since the flavor is the one thing that stayed.

Stress test

The blue in spirulina is a fragile protein, and a factory’s heat or a recipe’s acid breaks it down. Butterfly pea, a flower FDA cleared for cereal in 2025, held its color better in heat tests, though acid turns it purple. You can watch that happen at home by squeezing lemon into a cup of butterfly pea tea.

A few aisles over, Mars spent more than a decade on M&M’s without synthetic dyes and launched them missing blue and brown.

General Mills has lived through this once before, and the first to notice were children.

Trix lost blue and green once. They look gone again.

General Mills tried its new colors on brand-new cereals first, where, Food Dive noted, shoppers had no memory of how they should look. The It’s-It had forty years of it.
